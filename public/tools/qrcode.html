Tool Documentation & Usage Guide

A Quick Response (QR) code is a type of matrix barcode containing embedded binary info. It is widely used for scanning links, digital payments, and document indexing.

Best Practices for QR Generation:

  • Keep URLs Short: Shorter text segments create less dense QR square grids, making them significantly easier and faster for mobile cameras to scan.
  • High Contrast: Ensure your output keeps a high color contrast relative to the background paper or display panel. Black-on-white remains the gold standard.

Designing QR Codes That Actually Scan

A QR code is only useful if a phone can read it in the wild. The most overlooked requirement is the quiet zone: a blank margin of at least four modules on every side. Cropping that margin, or placing the code too close to other graphics, is the single most common reason a otherwise valid code fails to scan.

Size and placement come next. On print, aim for a minimum of roughly two centimeters across so the camera can resolve individual modules; on screens, make sure the code sits on a plain area away from video or animation behind it. If you embed a logo in the center, keep it under 15 percent of the area and rely on error correction to recover the obscured data.

Always test on real devices, not just your laptop. Scan with both iOS and Android cameras, at the distances your users will actually use, and under the lighting conditions of the real setting. A code that looks crisp on a monitor can wash out under bright sunlight on a poster.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Are online QR generators safe?
A: Many send your text to their servers, which is a risk for proprietary or internal links. Our tool generates codes 100 percent locally on your device, so nothing is uploaded.

Q: What is QR error correction?
A: It lets a code stay readable even if part is dirty or damaged. There are four levels, roughly recovering 7, 15, 25 and 30 percent of lost data. We use level M by default.

Q: What is the difference between static and dynamic QR codes?
A: A static code embeds the target directly and cannot be changed later. A dynamic code points to a short URL you can retarget, but it requires a server to resolve.

Q: Why keep the encoded text short?
A: Shorter data produces a less dense matrix, which mobile cameras scan faster and more reliably, especially at small print sizes.

Q: What is the minimum size to print a QR code?
A: Aim for at least about 2 centimeters per module at print resolution. Higher error-correction lets you print slightly smaller while staying scannable.
